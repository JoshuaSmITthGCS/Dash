# Designing and Validating a "Pre-Breakout" Stock Screen: A Research Base

## TL;DR

- **The strongest empirically-supported precursor signals are, in rank order: (1) earnings/revenue acceleration and post-earnings-announcement drift (PEAD), (2) the gross-profitability quality factor, (3) cross-sectional momentum and industry momentum, and (4) insider cluster buying — all documented in top-tier journals. "Early relative strength" and "volatility contraction/squeeze" are largely practitioner folklore with weak-to-no peer-reviewed support for their specific pre-breakout claims.**
- **For combining signals, the weight of quant evidence favors a weighted composite z-score / rank aggregation (O'Shaughnessy, Novy-Marx, Piotroski) over a strict "must-pass-all" checklist gate, because gating destroys breadth (Grinold-Kahn's Fundamental Law: IR = IC × √Breadth) and overfits to anecdotes; use gating only for hard liquidity/quality exclusions.**
- **Because nothing is backtested, validate PROSPECTIVELY with a rank-IC framework on point-in-time data. Expect to need roughly 1.5–3 years of monthly cross-sections for a typical single-factor IC (~0.03–0.05) to reach t≈2 significance, and benchmark the screen's realized "breakout hit rate" against a genuine null (per an endlessmetrics analysis of 2021, when the S&P 500 rose 26.9%, only 1,781 of 6,002 stocks beat the index — a ~70% chance a random stock underperformed even in a strong bull year), not an arbitrary target.**

## Key Findings

1. **Fundamental inflection is the best-evidenced precursor.** Earnings *acceleration* (the second derivative) is documented by He & Narayanamoorthy (2019/2020, *Journal of Accounting and Economics*) to produce ~1.8% market-adjusted returns over a one-month window and ~3.4% over a quarter — comparable to book-to-market, PEAD, and gross profitability anomalies. PEAD itself (Bernard & Thomas 1989/1990; Foster, Olsen & Shevlin 1984) drifts for ~60 trading days and persists up to four quarters. This gives you a *documented lead/lag structure* — the single most important asset for a "pre-breakout" thesis.
1. **Margin inflection maps to the gross-profitability factor** (Novy-Marx 2013, *JFE*), which per the paper has "roughly the same power as book-to-market predicting the cross-section of average returns" — but the evidence is for the *level* of gross profits-to-assets, not specifically for the *turn* (second derivative) in margins. The inflection claim is a reasonable extrapolation, not directly proven.
1. **Early relative strength is mostly practitioner folklore.** The academically-supported phenomenon is momentum (Jegadeesh & Titman 1993) and the "RS line new high before price" is an O'Neil/IBD/CANSLIM claim without independent peer-reviewed validation of the specific lead-time claim. It is plausibly a repackaging of momentum + PEAD, both of which ARE real.
1. **Volatility contraction (Bollinger squeeze / Minervini VCP) is the weakest link — the key falsifiable claim.** There is no peer-reviewed base rate for squeeze→genuine breakout. Practitioner "success rate" figures (55%, 60–75%, even "90.77%") are marketing with unstated methodology. The squeeze is non-directional by construction. Treat this as an unvalidated hypothesis to test, not an established signal.
1. **Insider cluster buying is well-supported** (Lakonishok & Lee 2001; Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski 2012; Kang, Kim & Wang 2018). Short interest predicts returns (Boehmer, Jones & Zhang 2008 — mainly the SHORT side / high short interest → negative returns), and industry momentum (Moskowitz & Grinblatt 1999) accounts for much of individual-stock momentum.
1. **Combine via composite, validate via rank-IC on point-in-time data, and beware backtest overfitting** (Bailey & López de Prado 2014, deflated Sharpe ratio).

## Details

### 1. Candidate Leading Indicators, Ranked by Strength of Evidence

**TIER A — Strong, top-journal empirical support**

**(a) Earnings / revenue growth ACCELERATION (second derivative).** He & Narayanamoorthy, "Earnings Acceleration and Stock Returns" (*Journal of Accounting and Economics*, 2020; SSRN 3057632) measure earnings acceleration as the change in earnings growth (seasonally-differenced EPS), on 377,907 firm-quarter observations across 8,824 firms, 1972–2015. A long-top-decile/short-bottom-decile strategy earned market-adjusted returns of **1.8% over a one-month window and 3.4% over a quarter-long window** (annualized ~23% monthly-window / ~14% quarter-window), robust to book-to-market, PEAD, and gross profitability controls. Crucially, the return predictability arises because "investors assuming a seasonal random walk model for quarterly earnings … miss predictable implications of earnings acceleration for future earnings growth two and three quarters hence" — i.e., a genuine *lead-time* mechanism. This is the strongest single anchor for a pre-breakout thesis. Note the counterweight: Chan, Karceski & Lakonishok (2003, *Journal of Finance*, "The Level and Persistence of Growth Rates") and Verdad's review find long-term earnings growth has little persistence and neither analyst forecasts nor valuation ratios reliably predict it — so acceleration is a *short-horizon* (1–3 quarter) signal, not a durable-growth signal.

**Revenue surprise specifically:** Jegadeesh & Livnat, "Revenue Growth and Stock Returns" documents that stock price reactions are significantly related to revenue surprises after controlling for earnings surprises, and that "increases in earnings accompanied by increases in revenues lead to more persistent earnings growth" — revenue-driven beats are higher-quality signals than earnings-only beats. This supports weighting revenue acceleration alongside earnings acceleration.

**(b) Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift (the lead/lag literature).** PEAD is what Fama (1998) called "the granddaddy of underreaction events." The canonical magnitude: Foster, Olsen & Shevlin (1984), replicated by Bernard & Thomas (1989, *Journal of Accounting Research* 27:1–36), estimate that over the **60 trading days (≈ one quarter)** following an announcement, a long-top-SUE-decile/short-bottom-decile position yields ~4.2% (≈25% annualized before costs); Bernard & Thomas's own estimate is nearer ~18–19% annualized. Attribution note: the "~4.2% over 60 days" figure is properly Foster, Olsen & Shevlin (1984), which Bernard & Thomas replicate. The drift is roughly symmetric — good-news stocks drift up ~2% and bad-news stocks down ~2% over 60 days. Bernard & Thomas (1990, *JAE*) show the drift **persists for approximately four quarters**, with abnormal returns clustering around the *subsequent* earnings announcement dates, and the top-minus-bottom spread was positive in 41 of 48 quarters (1974–1985). **Critical caveat for your track record:** PEAD has attenuated. A Columbia Business School working paper documents the hedge return declining from ~5% (1980s–90s) to ≤3% (late 2010s), and Martineau (2022, "Rest in Peace Post-Earnings Announcement Drift") finds multi-day PEAD is essentially non-existent for non-microcap stocks since ~2006. Kaczmarek & Zaremba (2025, *Finance Research Letters*) partially "revive" it with machine-learning on multi-quarter SUE history. **Implication:** the drift is real and gives you the lead-time mechanism, but its magnitude has shrunk and it is concentrated in smaller, less liquid names — exactly where implementation frictions bite.

**(c) Gross profitability / margin quality.** Novy-Marx (2013, *JFE* 108:1–28, "The Other Side of Value: The Gross Profitability Premium"), verbatim: "Profitability, measured by gross profits-to-assets, has roughly the same power as book-to-market predicting the cross-section of average returns… Controlling for gross profitability explains most earnings related anomalies, and a wide range of seemingly unrelated profitable trading strategies." Piotroski's FSCORE international evidence (Springer, 2000–2018 sample) confirms fundamental-strength composites. **Caveat:** this evidence is for the *level* of profitability, not specifically for the *turn* (margin inflection off a decline). Your margin-inflection hypothesis is a reasonable extension of Piotroski's ΔROA component (change in ROA is one of his nine signals and IS validated) but the specific "gross/operating margin turning up after a preceding decline" pattern is not directly proven in the literature and should be tested, not assumed.

**TIER B — Real but nuanced / conditional support**

**(d) Momentum & industry rotation.** Jegadeesh & Titman (1993, *Journal of Finance*) is the seminal result: buying 3–12 month winners and shorting losers earns ~1% per month. Moskowitz & Grinblatt (1999, *Journal of Finance*, "Do Industries Explain Momentum?") show industry momentum "accounts for much of the individual stock momentum anomaly" and is highly profitable using the largest, most liquid stocks — directly supporting a "sector/group rotation precedes individual moves" component. Wesley Gray & Jack Vogel's *Quantitative Momentum* and the frog-in-the-pan work — Da, Gurun & Warachka, "Frog in the Pan: Continuous Information and Momentum," *Review of Financial Studies* 27(7), July 2014, pp. 2171–2218 — show that the *path* matters: per the paper, "Momentum decreases monotonically from 5.94% for stocks with continuous information during their formation period to –2.07% for stocks with discrete information but similar cumulative formation-period returns." This is directly relevant: a stock quietly grinding higher on RS while price consolidates is the "frog-in-the-pan" setup — this is the one place where the "early RS" folklore has a genuine academic cousin.

**(e) Insider cluster buying.** Lakonishok & Lee (2001) found purchases more informative than sales, concentrated in small firms; Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski (2012, *Journal of Finance* 67:1009–1043, "Decoding Inside Information") split "routine" vs. "opportunistic" traders and found the opportunistic long-short portfolio earns "value-weight abnormal returns of 82 basis points per month (9.8 percent annualized, t=2.15), and equal-weight abnormal returns of 180 basis points per month (21.6 percent annualized, t=6.07)," while routine-trader returns are "essentially zero" (sample: Thomson Reuters insider filings, January 1986–December 2007). The paper further characterizes the signal: "The most informed opportunistic traders are local, nonexecutive insiders from geographically concentrated, poorly governed firms." Kang, Kim & Wang (2018, "Cluster Trading of Corporate Insiders") show cluster buys (within ~2 days of a peer insider) earn ~3.8% over 21 days vs. ~2% for solitary buys. A recent (2026) filing-date event study of 7,406 C-suite purchase signals found a modest +0.53% next-session and +1.01% five-session SPY-adjusted CAR (t ≈ 6.5 / 5.1) — the effect is real but smaller and faster than the older literature once you use the actual public filing date rather than the trade date. **Caveat:** the classic studies pre-date the post-2010 explosion in algorithmic trading and 10b5-1 plans, which compress the public-information window.

**(f) Short interest.** Boehmer, Jones & Zhang (2008), Asquith, Pathak & Ritter (2005), Diether, Lee & Werner (2009): *high* short interest predicts *negative* returns (short sellers are informed). The symmetric long-side claim — Boehmer, Huszar & Jordan (2009, "The Good News in Short Interest") — finds heavily-traded stocks with *low* short interest earn significant positive abnormal returns. So "short interest declining materially off highs" is best framed as *removal of an informed-seller headwind*, weakly supported; genuine short-squeeze dynamics are a distinct, more speculative mechanism. Asquith et al. also caution short interest in large stocks is not very informative.

**(g) Institutional ownership / 13F changes.** Evidence is genuinely mixed. Some find a positive relation (Gompers & Metrick), others none or negative (Dyakov et al. 2020, *International Review of Finance*: "either no or even a negative relationship between institutional trading and subsequent returns"). The 45-day 13F reporting lag limits its use as a *leading* indicator. Treat as low-weight/corroborative at best.

**TIER C — Folklore / weak or disputed for the specific pre-breakout claim**

**(h) Early relative strength (RS line new high before price).** This is a William O'Neil / IBD / CANSLIM practitioner construct (the "L" in CANSLIM, RS Rating ≥ 80). The claim that an RS-line new high *precedes* a price breakout with a measurable lead time is asserted across practitioner sources (Deepvue, TradingView, ChartMill) but I found **no independent peer-reviewed study** validating the specific lead-time claim. It is plausibly a visual repackaging of momentum + PEAD + frog-in-the-pan, all of which are real. Verdict: fold RS into the momentum component rather than treating it as an independent validated signal.

**(i) Volatility contraction / Bollinger squeeze / Minervini VCP.** *This is the key falsifiable claim to interrogate, and the evidence is weakest.* John Bollinger's own thesis is that low-volatility periods precede high-volatility periods — a statement about volatility clustering (which IS real, per the GARCH literature), NOT about *directional* breakouts. The squeeze is explicitly non-directional. Practitioner base-rate claims are unreliable: one blog cites "none of the Bollinger Bands methods exceed 55% success rates," others claim 60–75% "with filters," and one VCP source claims "90.77% success rates when major indices trade above their monthly 10-EMA" — all with unstated methodology and likely selection/marketing bias. **There is no peer-reviewed false-positive rate for squeeze→sustained breakout.** The honest position: volatility contraction may have value as a *timing/entry* refinement conditional on a fundamental catalyst being present, but on its own it is an unvalidated hypothesis. This is the component most likely to overfit to anecdotes (the "AMD/MSFT looked like this before it ran" trap).

### 2. Combining Fundamentals + Technicals Into One Score

**Composite/z-score vs. checklist gate — what the evidence says.** The quant literature broadly favors *weighted rank aggregation* over strict gating:

- **O'Shaughnessy (*What Works on Wall Street*) / O'Shaughnessy Asset Management** found a composite value factor "never dropped below the top three single valuation ratios in any" period and outperformed any single ratio, because combining "extracts the common driver … and allows the deficiencies of each individual multiple to wash out." This is the core argument for composites: they diversify signal-specific noise and avoid the value-trap failure mode of any one metric.
- **Piotroski (2000)** and its international replication (high-FSCORE firms outperform low by ~10%/year across markets) show that *summing binary signals* (a soft, additive scorecard) works — but note this is additive scoring, not a hard "must pass all nine" gate.
- **Novy-Marx (2013)** demonstrates the power of *combining* profitability with value ("controlling for profitability dramatically increases the performance of value strategies").
- **Stambaugh, Yu & Yuan (2015)** composite mispricing score (average rank across 11 anomalies) predicts returns better than components.

**The theoretical case against pure gating: the Fundamental Law of Active Management.** Grinold & Kahn (*Active Portfolio Management*): **IR = IC × √Breadth**, where IC is skill (forecast-return correlation) and Breadth is the number of independent bets. A strict "must pass ALL screens" gate collapses your universe to a handful of names, destroying Breadth and thus IR — even if each gate is individually informative. A weighted composite preserves Breadth by keeping the whole cross-section rankable. Ding & Martin (2017, *Journal of Empirical Finance*, "The Fundamental Law… Redux") and Qian & Hua (2004, "Active Risk and Information Ratio") refine this, adding the transfer coefficient (implementation shortfall) and IC volatility (strategy risk).

**Recommended architecture (hybrid):**

- **Gate only on hard exclusions** where the relationship is non-linear/disqualifying: minimum liquidity (dollar volume), minimum price, maximum bid-ask spread, and hard quality/solvency floors (e.g., not distressed by Altman Z). These are legitimate gates because they protect implementability, not because they are alpha signals.
- **Score everything else as a weighted composite of cross-sectional ranks (or winsorized z-scores):** a Fundamental Inflection sub-score (earnings acceleration, revenue acceleration, ΔROA/margin turn, SUE) + a Technical/Momentum sub-score (12-1 momentum, frog-in-the-pan smoothness, RS rank, volatility-contraction flag) + a Sentiment/Flow sub-score (insider cluster buys, short-interest change). CANSLIM is itself a hybrid — "growth first in the numbers, then in relative strength, then in volume-driven breakouts."
- **Weighting:** Start with equal-weight across sub-scores (equal-weighting is a robust default that resists overfitting — the whole O'Shaughnessy/Piotroski lesson). Do NOT optimize weights on the same data you validate on. If you later tune weights, do it on a separate in-sample period and hold out the rest.
- Winsorize (e.g., at 1st/99th percentile) before z-scoring to control the extreme-value sensitivity documented in the Pomona z-score study, and neutralize by sector/size so you are not just buying one hot industry.

### 3. Validation Methodology for a Prospective Screen

**3a. Rank-IC framework.** For each rebalance date t, compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation between your composite score and forward returns at 20/40/60-trading-day horizons. The rank (not Pearson) IC is preferred because it is robust to outliers and non-linearity. Collect the time series {IC_t}. Report mean IC, IC volatility, and the **ICIR = mean(IC)/std(IC)**. Documented equity factors have IC in the **0.02–0.05** range; anything persistently above ~0.05 monthly is strong.

**3b. Point-in-time data (non-negotiable).** Using today's restated fundamentals to backtest is the classic look-ahead bias: a firm reports Q2 in July, restates in September; a backtest that uses the September figure for an August decision is cheating. You must:

- Key fundamentals by **filing/availability date**, not fiscal-period-end date. Companies file 10-Ks weeks-to-months after period end. Use a point-in-time database (Compustat Point-in-Time / snapshot, or as-reported feeds) — index-membership survivorship and Compustat restatements are the two problems that "taught the industry the hard way."
- Use a **survivorship-bias-free universe** that includes delisted, bankrupt, and acquired names as of each historical date, not today's constituent list applied backward.
- **The lag test:** if shifting every input back one bar collapses the IC/Sharpe, you have look-ahead leakage somewhere. Every data point should carry two timestamps: effective date and availability date.

**3c. Prospective validation (since no backtest exists).** Run the screen live and log it. Statistical guidance from the Fundamental Law and Qian & Hua: the t-stat of your mean IC = **ICIR × √(N_periods)**. Concretely:

- Monthly IC mean 0.05, IC vol 0.10 → ICIR 0.50 → need N = (1.96/0.50)² ≈ **~16 months** for t ≈ 2.
- IC mean 0.05, IC vol 0.125 → ICIR 0.40 → **~24 months (~2 years)**.
- IC mean 0.05, IC vol 0.15 → ICIR 0.333 → **~35 months (~3 years)**.
- **Practical rule: budget 1.5–3 years of monthly cross-sections before your prospective IC estimate is trustworthy.** A real-world example from the quant literature: a published factor with mean IC 0.029 and IC std 0.157 (ICIR ≈ 0.185) reached IC t-stat 2.30 only over 155 months.
- You can accelerate evidence accumulation by increasing *breadth* (more names per period) and *frequency* (bi-weekly rather than monthly formation) — this is the Grinold-Kahn Breadth lever — but beware overlapping-horizon autocorrelation, which inflates apparent significance (use Newey-West standard errors for overlapping 20/40/60-day windows).
- Maintain a **live-tracking log**: timestamped picks, the composite score and sub-scores at pick time, the benchmark (sector-and-size-matched, e.g., equal-weight peer basket + the index), and forward returns at each horizon. This *is* your track record; it is also your out-of-sample test, immune to backtest overfitting because it was generated before the outcomes were known.

**3d. Base-rate benchmarking (the genuine null).** Judge the screen's hit rate against what pure chance would produce:

- **Group base rate:** Per an endlessmetrics analysis of 2021 (S&P 500 +26.9%), of a sample of 6,002 individual companies "only 1,781 performed better than a 26.9% return… the odds that it would have underperformed the S&P 500 is a (surprisingly large) 70%"; median stock +5%, mean +12%, 10th pct −57%, 90th pct +68%. Over a longer horizon, S&P Dow Jones Indices (Ganti) reports that across 2001–2020, "of the 980 stocks that were in the index during this period, only 211, or 22%, outperformed." So even in a *strong* single year a random stock had only ~30% odds of a >27% gain, and long-run outperformance is rarer still.
- **Index frequency:** the S&P 500 has gained ≥20% in ~1/3 of years and ≥25% in ~27% of years since 1926 — context for how unusual sustained large moves are.
- **Parametric null:** with ~20% annualized vol, quarterly σ ≈ 10%, so a +20–25% *quarterly* move on an average stock is ~2–2.5σ; single stocks with 40–60% idiosyncratic vol clear that bar far more often, which is why you MUST compute the base rate *within your specific filtered universe* (small/mid-cap high-vol names break out far more often by chance than large-caps). Define "breakout" operationally up front (e.g., +X% within N days AND close above a prior N-week high, held for M days to exclude one-day head-fakes) and compute the unconditioned base rate for that exact definition in that exact universe. The screen only has edge if its conditional hit rate materially exceeds this null.

### 4. Failure Modes Specific to This Screen Type

- **Value-trap-style false positives (fundamental inflections that reverse).** A single up-quarter in margin or growth mean-reverts often (Chan-Karceski-Lakonishok: growth doesn't persist). Mitigation: require *confirmation* (2+ consecutive quarters of acceleration, or acceleration + revenue confirmation per Jegadeesh-Livnat), and use the composite so one noisy signal can't carry a name.
- **"Correctly ignored" candidates (signal vs. noise / no catalyst).** Most earnings events produce no unusual reaction; the Caltech "Anomalous Anomaly" paper notes "practically all firm-specific drifts do not follow the portfolio drift path" — PEAD is a *portfolio-average* phenomenon, not a per-stock guarantee. A screen that fires on a fundamental turn the market rationally discounts (secularly declining industry, one-off gain) will be early because it is *wrong*, not early. Mitigation: require the fundamental signal to co-occur with at least one flow/attention confirmation (insider cluster buy, RS improvement, industry-momentum tailwind) before flagging.
- **Overfitting / backtest fragility.** This is the dominant risk. Bailey & López de Prado (2014, *Journal of Portfolio Management*, "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio") show that when you try many strategy variations and keep the best, the max Sharpe is inflated even if all candidates are pure noise. Their companion "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism" (2014, *Notices of the AMS*) and "The Probability of Backtest Overfitting" quantify this. Constructing a precursor combination that fits a handful of historical winners (the AMD/MSFT setup) is textbook overfitting. Mitigations: (1) use the **Deflated Sharpe Ratio** and adjust for the number of trials you ran; (2) apply **Minimum Backtest Length / Minimum Track Record Length** thinking; (3) prefer few, economically-motivated signals with equal weights over many optimized ones; (4) treat the prospective live log as the real out-of-sample test; (5) hold out a validation universe/period you never look at during design.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Design (weeks 1–4).** Lock a written spec BEFORE touching outcome data: (a) operational breakout definition (e.g., +20% within 60 trading days AND a close above the 40-day high sustained ≥5 days); (b) universe and hard gates (liquidity, price, solvency floor); (c) the composite = equal-weighted average of three sub-score ranks (Fundamental Inflection, Momentum/RS, Flow/Sentiment); (d) sub-score constituents drawn ONLY from Tier A/B signals above. Register this spec (git commit / timestamped doc) so you can later prove the live test was genuinely out-of-sample.

**Stage 1 — Base-rate calibration (weeks 2–6).** Using point-in-time, survivorship-free data, compute the *unconditioned* base rate of your exact breakout definition within your exact filtered universe, by year and by volatility bucket. This is your null hypothesis. If you cannot get point-in-time fundamentals, restrict v1 to price/volume/technical signals (which are point-in-time by nature) and add fundamentals only when you have proper data.

**Stage 2 — Prospective rank-IC logging (months 1–36).** Rebalance bi-weekly or monthly; log composite + sub-scores + forward 20/40/60-day returns; compute rolling mean IC, IC vol, ICIR, and IC t-stat. **Decision thresholds:** continue if after ~12 months the mean 60-day rank-IC is positive with ICIR trending > 0.3; escalate to a fundable strategy if by ~24–36 months the IC t-stat clears ~2.0 AND the conditional breakout hit rate exceeds the Stage-1 null by a margin that survives the Deflated Sharpe adjustment. **Kill/redesign** if mean IC is indistinguishable from zero at 18 months, or if the hit rate does not beat the null.

- *Benchmark that would change the plan:* if the fundamental-inflection sub-score carries all the IC and the volatility-contraction flag adds none (likely, given the weak evidence), drop the squeeze component — don't let folklore dilute a working composite.
- If IC is concentrated entirely in micro/small-caps (as PEAD now is), re-examine whether the screen is implementable net of spread/impact before believing it.

**Stage 3 — Robustness before any capital.** Compute Deflated Sharpe accounting for every variant you tried; run the lag test (shift inputs one bar); confirm results hold in a held-out sector/period; use Newey-West SEs for overlapping horizons. Only then treat the screen as "validated with a track record."

**Data/tooling:** point-in-time fundamentals (Compustat PIT or as-reported), survivorship-free price/delisting data (CRSP-style), Form 4 feed (EDGAR) for insider clusters, bi-weekly short-interest, and a GICS/industry map for momentum-neutralization.

## Caveats

- **Signal decay is real:** PEAD has shrunk by ~50%+ since the 1990s and is now concentrated in illiquid names; momentum has crashes; published anomalies routinely weaken post-publication (McLean & Pontiff). Your live IC will likely be lower than any published figure.
- **The volatility-contraction / squeeze component is the least-supported element** and is included as a *hypothesis to be tested*, not an established signal. Do not anchor the product's marketing on it until the prospective IC earns its place.
- **"Early relative strength" and RS-line-before-price are practitioner constructs** without independent peer-reviewed lead-time validation; they are folded into the (validated) momentum/frog-in-the-pan component rather than credited independently.
- **Practitioner "success rate" numbers** for VCP/Bollinger breakouts (55%–90%) come from marketing sources with unstated methodology and should not be cited as evidence.
- **Institutional 13F changes** have mixed evidence and a 45-day lag; keep low-weight.
- Nothing here is investment advice; it is screening-methodology research. Past-anomaly returns are historical measurements, not forward guarantees, and every figure should be re-estimated on your own point-in-time data before you rely on it.
